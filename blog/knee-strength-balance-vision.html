Research / Balance and strength

Strong knees, steady balance? What changes when vision disappears

Published: 8 November 2026 | Read time: 6 min | Biokineticum

A strong knee and a steady stance sound as though they should go together. But producing a large muscular effort and making the continuous small corrections needed to balance are not the same task.

A paper by Dariusz Mosler and Eryk Frankowski, published on 29 October 2026, explored that distinction in 30 physically active young men. With eyes open, maximal knee-extensor torque had almost no relationship with the measured sway. With eyes closed, higher torque was associated with a longer sway path.[1]

That is an intriguing finding—not a reason to avoid strength training. The study does not show that strength causes instability, diagnose a proprioceptive disorder or tell us who will be injured. Its value lies in showing why one impressive strength score cannot answer every question about movement.

Two instruments, two questions

The participants were recreationally active men, averaging 24.9 years of age, who reported at least four to six hours of weekly activity. Their activities included resistance training, team sports and running. They were not a sample of injured patients or exclusively elite athletes.

An AMTI force platform recorded 30-second single-leg stances with eyes open and eyes closed, on each leg. There was one trial per leg and condition: four stance trials per participant. The researchers tracked the centre of pressure (COP), the changing point at which the resultant ground-reaction force acts beneath the foot.

Think of COP path length as the length of a pen trace drawn by those pressure shifts. It is not the distance the person walked, the movement of the whole-body centre of mass, or a direct measurement of nerve function. A longer path describes more accumulated pressure movement during that particular task.

A Biodex dynamometer addressed a different question: how much turning effort could the knee muscles produce? Participants straightened and bent the knee against the machine at 60, 180 and 240 degrees per second. The reported peak torque, in newton-metres (N·m), describes a joint-turning effect, not force in newtons alone.

Both muscle groups were tested concentrically—while shortening. The study did not measure eccentric hamstring strength.

Removing vision changed the task dramatically

For the left leg, average COP path length rose from 1,655.4 mm with eyes open to 4,772.3 mm with eyes closed. That is 2.88 times the original value, or an increase of about 188%. The right-leg pattern was similar, rising from 1,641.4 to 4,885.9 mm.[1]

The distinction between “times as large” and “percentage increase” matters. A value near three times baseline is not a 300% increase; a 300% increase would mean four times baseline. The figures above use the paper’s tabulated means rather than its inconsistent percentage wording.

These are ratios of group means. They are not identical to averaging every participant’s individual eyes-closed/eyes-open ratio, which the paper also reports.

Removing visual information changes the information available for balance. The nervous system must rely on other inputs, including vestibular information from the inner ear and somatosensory information from the body. The observed change is consistent with that challenge. But the experiment did not isolate each sensory pathway or directly quantify how much weight the nervous system assigned to it.

Where strength entered the picture

The primary comparison concerned left knee-extensor peak torque at 60°/s and left-leg COP path length:

Stance conditionCorrelation with extensor torque
Eyes openPearson r = 0.007
Eyes closedPearson r = 0.546; 95% CI 0.231–0.757

Under eyes-closed conditions, the association remained significant after false-discovery-rate adjustment (adjusted p = 0.0108). Higher torque tended to accompany longer pressure paths. The confidence interval is fairly wide, reminding us that 30 people provide an estimate, not a universal constant.

Importantly, the researchers did more than contrast one significant correlation with one non-significant correlation. A repeated-measures mixed model formally tested whether the torque–sway relationship differed between visual conditions. The torque × condition interaction was 21.131 mm/(N·m), p = 0.0009. That coefficient describes a difference between statistical slopes. It is not a prediction that adding one N·m through training will cause an individual to sway another 21 mm.

The 60 repeated observations in that primary model came from 30 participants measured twice, not 60 independent people.

The body-size question is still open

There is a substantial caveat: the primary models used absolute torque, without adjusting for body mass. Participants weighed between 67 and 109 kg. Body size can influence mechanical measurements, so it deserves more than a passing mention.

The authors also examined torque against each person’s eyes-closed/eyes-open sway ratio. That comparison helps describe change relative to baseline, but a ratio does not automatically eliminate body-size confounding. It is not equivalent to including body mass as a model covariate or testing an appropriate scaling method.

The cautious conclusion is that the condition-dependent association was observed in this sample. Its independence from body size, and the mechanisms behind it, remain unresolved. Ideas about stiffness or control strategies are possible explanations, not directly demonstrated mechanisms here.

Interesting quadrants are not diagnoses

The paper arranged participants into four groups using knee-extensor torque of 270 N·m and an eyes-closed/eyes-open ratio of 3.00 as boundaries. These came from the sample’s distributions. They were not externally validated clinical cut-offs.

A graph like this can make contrasting profiles visible. It cannot establish that someone above a line has damaged proprioception, is unsafe to train, or has an elevated probability of an ACL injury. Moving across the boundary might reflect a small measurement difference rather than a meaningful change in health.

The study also found that conventional concentric hamstrings-to-quadriceps ratios varied with testing speed. That is another reminder that a test result belongs to its protocol, not to a context-free “ideal” number. Neither those ratios nor a 10% between-leg difference should be promoted from this study into a personal injury forecast.

What this changes—and what it does not

For a coach or clinician, the useful question is: does this test measure the ability I am actually interested in? A dynamometer measures knee performance under controlled conditions. A one-leg stance challenges postural control. Neither alone reproduces an unexpected landing, a change of direction or a sporting collision.

No participants were followed for future injuries. No training or rehabilitation programme was compared. The paper therefore cannot establish injury prediction, choose an effective intervention or validate return-to-sport clearance. Its findings also need independent testing in women, older adults, adolescents, clinical populations and elite competitors.

Nor is this a home-testing prescription. Unsupervised eyes-closed single-leg challenges are not needed to apply the article’s message, and a home webcam does not reproduce the force-platform and dynamometer measurements used here.

Better questions before bigger conclusions

The next steps are research questions: does the pattern persist with body-size adjustment, repeated balance trials and larger independent samples? Does it relate to dynamic sporting tasks? Could any proposed profile predict a meaningful outcome prospectively?

Until those questions are tested, the strongest takeaway is straightforward: strength and balance provide different information, and changing the visual conditions can change their observed relationship. More measurements are useful only when their limits remain visible.
