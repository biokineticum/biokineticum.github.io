Martial arts bibliotherapy: reading as aggression therapy support

Published: 9 September 2026 | Read time: 7 min | Dr. hab. Dariusz Mosler

Screens teach aggression efficiently. Two 2015 texts — one full Arch Budo article (11:353–359) and one conference paper in the Health and Martial Arts proceedings — sketch martial arts bibliotherapy as a sub-tool of prophylactic and therapeutic agonology.

What the concept claims

Curated literary narratives of struggle (including inner struggle) can be reinterpreted with agonology’s vocabulary and paired with cognitive-behavioural work on aggressiveness. One strand asks whether bibliotherapy can partly compensate continuous “education for aggression” from electronic media and harsh interpersonal patterns in children and adults. The other frames bibliotherapy as prospective support for CBT-based aggressiveness therapy — inspiration and structure, not a solo cure.

How to use without folklore

Build reading lists that make conflict, restraint and honour discussable; run guided reflection; measure behaviour change with proper tools (see also the Fair Play validation post). Do not replace psychiatry or psychotherapy with a samurai novel.

Series link

Sits beside the judo–agonology public-health review: same research programme, softer delivery channel.

Prophylactic and therapeutic agonology treats struggle as an object of science — including inner struggle narrated in literature. Nobel-adjacent combat themes in later bibliotherapy writing show how high literature can feed the same toolkit; the 2015 pair is the programme’s early charter for aggression and media.

Electronic media’s continuous modelling of aggression is the problem statement, not a nostalgia rant. Interpersonal aggression in families and peer groups is the second front. Bibliotherapy is proposed as partial compensation and as CBT adjunct — always “support”, never “instead”.

Cite both 2015 items when you build a reading-and-discussion module beside behavioural work.

Where this sits in the lab

Biokineticum’s public notes exist so practitioners can reach the DOI without drowning in jargon — and so athletes, teachers and clinicians see that combat-sport science also publishes on prisons, faith, fair play, sauna physiology and disability policy. Soft topics still deserve hard methods: sample sizes, instruments, effect directions and explicit limits.

If you teach, treat or coach from these findings, copy the reference list into your syllabus, open the full text, and write down what you will measure next week. That is the only CTA that always fits.

Reading notes

Psychosocial and public-health papers describe associations in defined samples — not moral verdicts about individuals. We keep the language descriptive, avoid stigma, and point you to the DOI for methods and limits.

If the topic touches your clinic, classroom or programme design, treat this post as a map to the paper, not a substitute for it.
