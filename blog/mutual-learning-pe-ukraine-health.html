Mutual learning in PE: Ukrainian students’ health indicators moved

Published: 9 September 2026 | Read time: 8 min | Dr. hab. Dariusz Mosler

Physical education can be a lecture with burpees — or a place where students teach each other. In this 2019 study, experimental groups (men n = 31, women n = 33) received 64 PE lessons built around intra-subgroup interaction, rotating roles and peer checking of skills. Control groups (men 32, women 31) kept traditional lessons.

What improved

Somatic health markers included Ketle, Robinson, lifetime and strength indices; physical preparedness used running tests, sit-ups and medicine-ball throws; mental work used Schultz–Platonov tables (processing efficiency, speed of figuring out work, mental stability). Results pointed to increased functional capacity of cardiovascular and respiratory systems in both sexes and a meaningful lift in physical and mental fitness under the mutual-learning programme — with a clear cardiovascular signal.

Pedagogy over slogans

Mutual learning is not “less teaching”. It is structured peer accountability. If your faculty still treats PE as disposable credit, this paper is ammunition: health indicators moved when the method changed.

Limits

University cohorts in Ukraine; programme fidelity matters. Replicate before declaring a national syllabus rewrite.

Academic load above 36–40 hours weekly and uneven daily schedules are the backdrop the introduction names — PE as counterweight, not decoration. Mutual learning forced students to articulate cues, spot errors and own each other’s progress across 64 contacts.

Robinson and related indices speaking to cardiovascular function give physicians and PE staff a shared dashboard. Schultz–Platonov mental-work tables remind us that “fitness” in students includes cognitive stamina under time pressure.

If your university still grades PE only on attendance sweat, this design is a ready alternative with published health-marker movement in both sexes.

Where this sits in the lab

Biokineticum’s public notes exist so practitioners can reach the DOI without drowning in jargon — and so athletes, teachers and clinicians see that combat-sport science also publishes on prisons, faith, fair play, sauna physiology and disability policy. Soft topics still deserve hard methods: sample sizes, instruments, effect directions and explicit limits.

If you teach, treat or coach from these findings, copy the reference list into your syllabus, open the full text, and write down what you will measure next week. That is the only CTA that always fits.

Reading notes

Psychosocial and public-health papers describe associations in defined samples — not moral verdicts about individuals. We keep the language descriptive, avoid stigma, and point you to the DOI for methods and limits.

If the topic touches your clinic, classroom or programme design, treat this post as a map to the paper, not a substitute for it.
