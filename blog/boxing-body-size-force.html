Bigger boxers, bigger force? What a jump test can—and cannot—tell us

Published: 8 November 2026 | Read time: 6 min | Biokineticum

A boxer steps onto a force plate, jumps, and produces an impressive peak-force number. Does that mean better explosiveness? A harder punch? Or simply a bigger athlete pushing against the ground?

Those are different questions. A study published on 3 November 2026, with Dariusz Mosler as first author, makes the distinction particularly clear. In 34 professional male boxers, body-size indicators were associated with several measures of absolute lower-body force. They were much less informative about jump height and other qualities. Crucially, the study did not measure a single punch.[1]

The useful message is not “size does not matter”. It is that the number on the force plate needs a label before it becomes a sporting story.

What happened in the laboratory?

The researchers recruited a convenience sample of professional male boxers in Mexico City. Their average age was 24.21 years, but the range was 16.21–34.21 years: five participants were younger than 18. This was not an adult-only sample, nor a representative survey of professional boxing.

Each athlete completed anthropometric measurements and three lower-body test protocols within one day:

  • A countermovement jump (CMJ): a downward movement followed by a vertical jump, with hands on hips.
  • A separate countermovement rebound jump: an initial jump followed by a rapid rebound after landing.
  • An isometric squat: pushing as hard and as fast as possible against a fixed bar, without lifting it.

A force plate recorded mechanical outputs. Body mass, height, skinfolds, girths and bone breadths described physique. Muscle, adipose and bone masses were estimated from anthropometric equations, not measured directly by imaging. Those estimates should not be treated as precisely weighed tissue compartments.

The design was cross-sectional: a snapshot of differences between people, not a programme that changed their bodies or training.

Force is not the same thing as jump height

Imagine two athletes producing the same upward acceleration. The heavier athlete requires more net force because there is more mass to accelerate. A bigger force reading therefore need not translate into a higher jump.

The study’s CMJ peak-force variable was the vertical ground-reaction force during propulsion, without subtracting body weight. That detail matters when comparing athletes of different sizes. Jump height answers a different question: how much take-off speed was achieved, which depends on net impulse relative to body mass—not simply the highest force reached at one instant.

Other outputs were different again. Rate of force development described how quickly force rose during the first 100 milliseconds of the isometric squat, not the jump. Reactive indices described jump performance in relation to time. None is a synonym for “punching power”.

What actually tracked with what?

The clearest headline associations included:

Pair of measurementsSpearman correlation, ρ
Body mass and CMJ peak force0.663
Estimated muscle mass and CMJ peak force0.573
Estimated muscle mass and isometric-squat peak force0.638

These are positive rank correlations: athletes higher on one measure tended to rank higher on the other. A correlation of 0.663 does not mean a 66.3% improvement, and it does not predict what will happen if an individual gains a kilogram.

BMI and corrected girths also correlated with CMJ peak force. Estimated adipose mass did too. That last result is a useful warning against turning every association into advice: it is not evidence that adding body fat improves boxing performance. Several size-related measures can move together.

By contrast, associations of anthropometry with CMJ height, early rate of force development and the dynamic strength index did not survive the study’s multiple-testing correction. For body mass and CMJ height, the observed correlation was just 0.071. This does not prove there can never be a relationship; it shows why physique is an inadequate substitute for measuring the quality of interest.

The most important statistical brake

The researchers examined 117 correlations. Testing many pairs creates opportunities for chance findings, so they used the Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure. Thirteen associations survived that correction. FDR reduces a multiple-testing problem; it does not turn an exploratory association into a causal effect.

They also asked a harder question: did other physique measures remain associated with absolute force after accounting for body mass? In a separate, exploratory analysis of 24 mass-adjusted associations, none survived FDR correction.

That does not establish that muscle has no independent contribution. With 34 participants and overlapping size measures, uncertainty remains. But it does mean that these results cannot support a claim that estimated muscle mass predicts force independently of overall body size.

The authors also did not formally test whether one correlation was larger than another. “The observed pattern favoured absolute-force measures” is fair; “the study proved one relationship is stronger” goes too far.

What coaches can take away—without rewriting the training plan

The defensible practical lesson is about interpretation, not a new exercise prescription.

First, ask which quality was measured. Absolute force, force relative to body mass, jump height and rapid force production describe different aspects of performance. A large value in one column cannot fill the others by assumption.

Second, preserve context: weight category, protocol, device and repeated-trial variability. The paper’s quadrant plots used this sample’s medians to organise the dots. They were not validated athlete types or thresholds for assigning training.

Third, leave a gap between a general laboratory test and a sport-specific outcome. Lower-body mechanics are relevant to the whole-body action of boxing, but this experiment did not establish transfer to punch force, accuracy, fatigue resistance or competitive success. It tested no training intervention and provides no basis for recommending weight gain or weight cutting.

A useful study, not a final verdict

The sample was small, male-only, recruited by availability and mixed in age and weight category. Some outcomes relied on manufacturer-exported calculations whose exact signal processing could not be reconstructed. The isometric force export also did not independently establish whether body weight had been included or subtracted, limiting comparisons with other systems.

The published analysis documents corrections to source-spreadsheet formulas and derived estimates. That transparency is useful, but it does not remove the uncertainty of prediction equations or missing raw device traces.

The takeaway is deliberately narrower than a knockout headline: bigger boxers often registered greater absolute lower-body force; their body measurements did not tell the whole performance story. To understand a jump, measure a jump. To understand a punch, measure a punch.
